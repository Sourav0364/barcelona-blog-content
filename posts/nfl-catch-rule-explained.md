**What counts as a catch in the NFL?** NFL Football Operations describes a catch through three elements: the player secures control before the ball touches the ground, touches the ground inbounds with both feet or another part of the body, and then performs an act common to the game—or maintains control long enough to do so.

## The NFL’s three-part catch test

The [NFL Football Operations glossary](https://operations.nfl.com/rules-officiating/nfl-football-basics/football-terms) explains that a forward pass is complete when the player satisfies the catch requirements. In plain language, follow these three parts:

1. **Control:** The player secures the ball in the hands or arms before it touches the ground.
2. **In-bounds contact:** The player touches the ground inbounds with both feet, or with another body part besides the hands.
3. **A football act or sustained control:** After the first two parts, the player performs an act common to the game—such as tucking the ball, taking an additional step, turning upfield, or avoiding an opponent—or keeps control long enough to do so.

A phrase like “he got a foot down” is only one detail. To apply the NFL catch rule, officials consider the full sequence and whether the player completed the required elements. The glossary’s examples also show why there is no need to reduce every catch to a fixed number of steps.

## Catch vs. incomplete pass

A pass is not complete just because the ball appears to touch a receiver’s hands. If the player does not establish control, make the required in-bounds contact, or complete the football act/control element, the completed-catch standard has not been met. Conversely, an in-bounds reception that satisfies all three parts is a completed pass.

For a sideline play, check the in-bounds contact carefully: the glossary specifies both feet or another body part touching inbounds. A single still frame may not show whether control was secured before the ball reached the ground or what happened immediately afterward, so the sequence matters more than one isolated moment.

The [2026 NFL Rulebook](https://operations.nfl.com/rules-officiating/2026-nfl-rulebook) is the official reference for current game rules. When a ruling is close, use the full play and the league’s rule language rather than relying on a short clip or a simplified “three steps” description.

## Applying the rule to an NFL game

When watching a pass play, ask three questions in order: Did the player control the ball? Did the player establish the required in-bounds contact? After that, did the player make a football act or maintain control long enough? That checklist helps separate a completed reception from a pass that does not meet the catch standard.

A reception and a touchdown are also not the same question. First determine whether the player completed a catch; then follow where the ball and player went under the scoring rules. The catch definition is the starting point for understanding the play, not a substitute for every rule that may apply near a boundary or goal line.

## NFL catch-rule FAQ

### Does a receiver need two feet inbounds for an NFL catch?

The NFL glossary says the player must touch the ground inbounds with both feet or another part of the body besides the hands. The rest of the catch requirements still apply.

### What does “football move” mean?

NFL Football Operations gives examples such as tucking the ball, extending it forward, taking an additional step, turning upfield, or avoiding an opponent. The definition also allows for maintaining control long enough to perform an act common to the game.

### Does a catch automatically mean a touchdown?

No. A catch determines whether the pass was completed. The play’s result depends on what happens next and the applicable scoring rules.

## The takeaway

The NFL catch rule is a sequence: control the ball, meet the in-bounds requirement, then complete a football act or maintain control long enough to do so. Follow all three elements to understand why a pass is ruled complete or incomplete.
